# Decision Log

Technical decisions and deviations for **Delivery SLA — Olist Marketplace**.

The project was rebuilt partway through onto a different dataset. Decisions taken before that
rebuild are kept in the second section for the record, but they describe a superseded build and
do not apply to the current one.

## Current project

| Date | Decision | Reason | Impact |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2026-10-04 | Replaced the generated dataset with the Brazilian E-Commerce Public Dataset by Olist, pulled from Kaggle. | The project owner asked for real transactional data rather than data produced by a generator. Olist carries a real promised-delivery date alongside the actual delivery timestamp, which is what makes a genuine SLA analysis possible. | `generate_data.py`, `data/raw/` and every query were rebuilt. The original synthetic framing and the prescription-verification analysis are gone, since neither exists in the source. The previous build is kept on the `synthetic-version` branch. |
| 2026-10-04 | On-time is evaluated by comparing **calendar dates**, not timestamps. | `order_estimated_delivery_date` is recorded at 00:00:00 for all 99,441 rows, so a timestamp comparison marks an order delivered on its promised day as late. That affects 1,292 orders and 1.34 percentage points. | On-time rate is 93.23% rather than 91.89%. Durations are still computed from timestamps; only the comparison is date-granular, and every query header states this. |
| 2026-10-04 | Delivery-partner analysis is rebuilt around sellers, and refund analysis around review scores. | Olist has no courier or refund fields. Sellers are the only fulfilment-side dimension available, and the review score is the only recorded customer outcome. | 84 sellers carry 200 or more eligible orders, with late rates from 1.78% to 23.20%, so the spread supports a ranking. Review score replaces refund value as the cost-of-lateness measure. |
| 2026-10-04 | Data-quality rules are derived from defects already present in the source rather than injected. | The dataset ships with genuine problems: 775 orders with no items, 166 impossible carrier dates, 61 deliveries recorded before approval, 261,831 duplicate geolocation rows. | The issue log is reconciled against an independent recount of the raw files instead of a generator manifest. |
| 2026-10-04 | `data/external/` is gitignored rather than committed. | The extracted dataset is 164 MB and is third-party licensed; committing it would bloat the repository and redistribute someone else's data. | The README documents `python database/fetch_data.py` to obtain it. Derived outputs under `data/processed/` stay committed so the analysis is readable without running the pipeline. |
| 2026-10-04 | On-time is evaluated at date granularity: DATE(Delivered_Ts) <= DATE(Estimated_Ts), never a timestamp comparison. | order_estimated_delivery_date is stored at 00:00:00 on all 99,441 rows, so the promise is a calendar day. A timestamp comparison marks a parcel handed over at 14:00 on its promised day as late. | On-time rate is 93.23% over 96,470 SLA-eligible orders instead of 91.89%, a 1.34 point gap across 1,292 orders. The rule is stated in common.py, in the schema header and in every query header, and 01_data_quality.sql recomputes the stored flag from it in both orders and fact_orders. |
| 2026-10-04 | Durations are timestamp differences in hours, deliberately finer than the date-level promise. | Hour-level lags are what diagnose where time is lost; only the SLA verdict is a calendar question. | Avg delivery duration 301.40 h against a mean promise of 569.67 h; mean delay among late orders 271.25 h. Mixing the two bases is the single easiest way to break the headline number, so the schema says so in a comment. |
| 2026-10-04 | Duration decomposition: Approval_Hours and Handoff_Hours are measured from the purchase timestamp, Transit_Hours from the carrier handoff, so Handoff_Hours + Transit_Hours = Actual_Delivery_Hours. | Measuring the handoff from approval instead would make it negative on 1,359 orders where the seller ships before payment approval settles, which is ordinary rather than defective. Anchoring both lags on purchase keeps every component non-negative and additive. | Approval lag is a sub-interval of the handoff lag rather than a disjoint stage. Any stage-by-stage chart has to say so; the schema header carries the identity. |
| 2026-10-04 | Two defects found during profiling were added to the briefed rule list: delivery timestamp before the carrier handoff (DQ-09, 23 orders) and cancelled orders carrying a delivery timestamp (DQ-10, 6 orders). | Both are real incoherences in the published tables and both would otherwise flow silently into transit-time and status cuts. | Transit lag is nulled on the 23; the 6 cancelled orders keep both values and stay outside the SLA denominator, which filters on status. |
| 2026-10-04 | Multi-payment orders were added as their own rule (DQ-12, 2,961 orders) alongside the briefed multi-seller rule. | fact_orders carries a single Payment_Type and Payment_Installments, so the same attribution ambiguity exists and needed the same documented resolution. | Primary payment is the largest payment value, ties broken on payment sequence. Any payment-type cut inherits that convention for 3.0% of orders. |
| 2026-10-04 | Impossible sequences null the derived lag, never the timestamp. | Deciding which of two timestamps is wrong would be a guess. On the 61 orders delivered before approval the carrier date corroborates the delivery, so the approval lag is the suspect value; nulling the delivery timestamp would also move the headline SLA rate. | Orders stay in every count. 221 orders have no approval lag, 1,949 no handoff lag, 2,989 no transit lag, and the Approval_Bucket reads Unknown wherever the lag is gone. |
| 2026-10-04 | The SLA flags (Is_Delivered, Is_Sla_Eligible, Is_On_Time, Is_Late) are computed once in clean_data.py and stored in the cleaned orders table; SQL re-derives them and asserts agreement rather than being the only definition. | One definition in one place is what keeps the dashboard, workbook and queries from drifting apart, but the date rule is the project's most consequential choice and must be visible and verifiable in SQL. | 01_data_quality.sql fails if a stored flag disagrees with DATE(Delivered_Ts) <= DATE(Estimated_Ts), in orders and again in fact_orders, so a wrong rewrite of query 07 cannot pass the gate. |
| 2026-10-04 | Primary-item and primary-payment attribution is materialised as Is_Primary_Item and Is_Primary_Payment flags in the cleaned child tables. | Resolving the ambiguity once in the cleaning step leaves no room for query 07 to pick a different tiebreak, and a post-load check can assert exactly one flagged row per order. | Primary item is the highest-priced item, ties broken on item number; primary payment is the largest value, ties broken on sequence. Seller_Count keeps the multi-seller ambiguity visible in fact_orders. |
| 2026-10-04 | Reviews are physically deduplicated to one row per order in the cleaned table, keeping the latest answered review with review id as the tiebreak. | Review score is a one-per-order attribute in fact_orders, and a stable tiebreak is what makes reruns byte-identical now that there is no seed. | 551 of 99,224 review rows are dropped and logged; order_reviews has a single-column primary key, so a duplicate can never reappear through the loader. |
| 2026-10-04 | Geolocation is deduplicated and then collapsed to one row per zip prefix, using the mean coordinate and the modal city and state. | The zip prefix is the only level at which the table joins to customers and sellers, and the deduplicated table is 738,332 rows and roughly 45 MB, which does not belong in a repository. | 19,015 rows. It locates a prefix, not an address, which is stated in the report's limitations. The 261,831 removed duplicates are logged as a single summary row because the table carries no order key. |
| 2026-10-04 | Determinism now comes from stable sorting rather than a seed: every processed CSV is sorted on its primary key with a stable sort before writing, with fixed float and datetime formats. | There is no sampling left in the pipeline, so the only reproducibility risk is row ordering out of groupby and drop_duplicates. | Running clean_data.py twice produces byte-identical processed CSVs, verified by hash from a cold start. |
| 2026-10-04 | Products keep both the Portuguese category name and the English translation; the English column is null where the translation table has no entry. | Two categories (pc_gamer and portateis_cozinha_e_preparadores_de_alimentos) are missing from the translation file, and dropping the Portuguese name would make those rows untraceable. | 623 of 32,951 products have no English category: 610 carry no category at all and 13 fall in the two untranslated ones. Category cuts should disclose the null bucket rather than drop it. |
| 2026-10-04 | The database is rebuilt as database/olist.db and the cleaned tables are loaded parents-first with foreign keys enforced. | The schema now has real dimension tables, so insert order matters and the FKs are worth enforcing at load time rather than documenting. | load_data.py asserts each table's loaded row count against its CSV and fails the run on any mismatch. |
| 2026-10-04 | Queries 02-06 read the cleaned source tables directly rather than fact_orders. | run_queries.py executes sql/*.sql in filename order and fact_orders is empty until 07 runs, so an earlier query reading it would return nothing on a cold rebuild. | Each analysis query is self-contained and the build order carries no hidden dependency. The SLA flags are read from orders, where query 01 validates them against the date rule. |
| 2026-10-04 | 04_seller_analysis.sql and 06_review_analysis.sql declare their shared join as a temporary view instead of repeating a CTE in every statement. | A CTE is scoped to one statement, and both files emit four or six result sets off the same order-to-seller or order-to-review join. Repeating a fifteen-line CTE six times is the kind of duplication that drifts. | Each file opens with CREATE TEMP VIEW and closes with DROP VIEW, so the view lives only for the connection run_queries.py opens and nothing persists in olist.db. |
| 2026-10-04 | Seller late rates are reported on the >=200-eligible-order cohort as the headline, with all 2,959 sellers in the output and ranking suppressed below 30. | A late rate on 30 orders is rankable but not plannable. The 84-seller cohort covers 42.34% of eligible volume and is where a rate is stable enough to set a service bar against. | The scorecard keeps every seller visible with its n; the cohort summary in result set 3 carries the min, median and max that the findings report quotes. |
| 2026-10-04 | The approval-lag confound is addressed by banding Promised_Delivery_Hours into calendar weeks (<=14d, 15-21d, 22-28d, >28d) rather than into quartiles. | Quartile boundaries move whenever the data is refreshed and mean nothing to an operator. Week bands are stable and readable, and every resulting cell holds at least 774 orders. | The within-band cut shows the >24h bucket worst in all four bands, with the gap shrinking from 4.04 to 1.07 points as the promise widens - a consistent gradient the pooled cut had hidden. |
| 2026-10-04 | Insight 1 attributes the Feb-Mar 2018 collapse to transit rather than to seller handoff, and stops there. | Avg_Transit_Hours roughly doubles in those months while Avg_Handoff_Hours barely moves, which locates the delay after carrier pickup. The dataset holds no carrier, weather or capacity field, so the cause cannot be identified. | The finding is stated as a location, not a diagnosis, and the limitation says explicitly that a carrier disruption, a warehouse migration and a demand spike would all look identical in these columns. |
| 2026-10-04 | The seller late-rate range is reported as 0.89% to 19.07%, not the 1.78% to 23.20% quoted in the phase brief. | The briefed range reproduces exactly when the on-time test is run on raw timestamps instead of calendar dates. The canonical rule is the date comparison, and query 01 fails the build if fact_orders disagrees with it. | Same 84-seller cohort either way; only the rate changes. Every seller figure in the report and the outputs uses the date rule, so they are internally consistent and gate-clean. |

## Superseded — before the dataset change

These decisions belong to the earlier build, which generated its own data and modelled a different
domain. None of the tables, columns or metrics they mention exist in the current project: there is
no generator, no prescription verification, no refund field and no delivery-partner dimension. They
are retained because deleting the reasoning behind a discarded approach would misrepresent how the
project actually developed. That build is preserved on the `synthetic-version` git branch.

| Date | Decision | Reason | Impact |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2026-10-04 | Project lives in `delivery-sla-olist-marketplace/`; generator, cleaning and query-runner scripts sit in `database/` next to the loader, `run_all.py` at repo root. | Keeps every script that touches the database or its source CSVs in one place; a single entry point at root is what a reviewer looks for first. | Paths in all scripts resolve from a `ROOT` constant in `database/common.py`, so cwd does not matter. |
| 2026-10-04 | Pinned pandas 3.0.6, numpy 2.5.3, openpyxl 3.1.5 in `requirements.txt`. | Versions resolved in the build environment (Python 3.12.10, Windows 11). Pinning keeps regeneration byte-identical across machines. | pandas 3.x drops several 1.x-era APIs; scripts use the current API only. |
| 2026-10-04 | SQL executes through Python's `sqlite3` module rather than a `sqlite3` CLI. | No `sqlite3` CLI on the build machine, and requiring one would add an install step to the one-command reproduction. | `database/run_queries.py` reads each `sql/*.sql` file and writes its result to `data/processed/query_outputs/`. |
| 2026-10-04 | README documents reproduction as `py run_all.py` with `python run_all.py` as the alternative. | On the build machine a bare `python` resolves to the Microsoft Store alias stub; the `py` launcher reaches the real 3.12.10 install. | No code change — a documentation note only. |
| 2026-10-04 | Generator encodes tendencies, not conclusions: partner reliability is a median transit time and log-sigma per partner and city tier, plus a monsoon congestion factor on Jun-Aug. | The brief forbids hardcoding findings. Rate parameters let the on-time spread across partners and cities fall out of sampling. | Overall on-time rate lands at 83.21% over 2,740 SLA-eligible orders, with partners spanning 63.08% to 94.06%. Re-tuning a parameter changes the finding, which is the point. |
| 2026-10-04 | Delivery clock modelled as verification_lag + dispatch_prep + transit, all measured from Order_Date. | Keeps prescription verification a component inside the promised window rather than an addition on top, matching the canonical definition. | The verification-time vs delay relationship is mechanical, so Query 5 measures something real instead of a painted-on correlation. |
| 2026-10-04 | Promised_Delivery_Hours grid: Tier 1 OTC 24 h, Tier 1 Chronic 48 h, Tier 2 OTC 48 h, Tier 2 Chronic 72 h. | Uses all three allowed promise values and reflects that a prescription order needs a verification step before dispatch. | Breach rates are comparable within a tier/category cell; cross-cell comparisons must hold the promise constant. |
| 2026-10-04 | Order_Status to Delivery_Status mapping: Completed covers Delivered and the In Transit tail at the 2025-09-30 snapshot; Refunded covers Delivered-and-refunded plus Returned (RTO); Cancelled has no deliveries row. | The three delivery states need an unambiguous home in the order lifecycle, and orders placed near the data cut-off are genuinely still moving. | 19 In Transit and 15 Returned rows carry NULL Actual_Delivery_Hours by design and sit outside the SLA denominator. Not a data-quality defect, so they are not in the issue log. |
| 2026-10-04 | Rejected and Pending prescriptions both resolve to Order_Status = 'Cancelled'. Pending keeps a submitted time with NULL verified time and NULL minutes. | A rejected prescription cannot be dispatched, and a verification that never completes is auto-cancelled when the window lapses. Pending has no duration to record. | 60 Rejected and 41 Pending orders are cancelled. Rx cancellation analysis buckets them by verification time only where minutes exist; Pending falls in an Unknown bucket. |
| 2026-10-04 | Refund Rate is computed with numerator and denominator both restricted to Delivery_Status = 'Delivered'; Total Refund INR covers every refunded row including the 15 Returned parcels. | The canonical denominator is delivered orders, and RTO refunds are not delivered orders - counting them in the numerator alone would overstate the rate. | Refund rate reads 5.55% (153 of 2,756 delivered). Total refund value is INR 116,134.28, of which the Returned parcels are a disclosed slice. Refund incidence and refund amount stay separate metrics. |
| 2026-10-04 | Customer_City is an attribute of the customer, not of the individual order. | DQ-04 imputation from the customer's other orders is only defensible if a customer has one city. | 14 of the 20 missing cities impute unambiguously; the 6 single-order customers get 'Unknown' with a NULL City_Tier and are excluded from city rankings. |
| 2026-10-04 | DQ-08 reconciles Order_Status alongside Refund_Flag: a positive Refund_Amount sets the order to Refunded. | Trusting the amount but leaving the order marked Completed would leave the two tables disagreeing and fail the post-load consistency checks. | 5 of the 10 DQ-08 rows move to Refunded. Still one issue-log row per affected delivery row, so the manifest reconciliation is unchanged. |
| 2026-10-04 | DQ-10 is detected on the raw NULL duration before DQ-05 nulls out-of-range values. | Otherwise the 10 impossible durations would be logged twice, once under each rule, and the manifest reconciliation would silently absorb it. | DQ-05 logs 10 and DQ-10 logs 6, matching the manifest exactly. 16 delivered orders end up without a usable duration and are disclosed. |
| 2026-10-04 | Excluding the 5 DQ-02 orders orphans their 10 child rows, which are then logged under DQ-03 rather than netted out of the expected count. | The cascade is a real consequence of the exclusion, not a second injected defect; hiding it would make the reconciliation assertion meaningless. | Expected DQ-03 count is computed as 4 injected plus the cascade, and clean_data.py raises if the issue log disagrees. |
| 2026-10-04 | database/run_queries.py exports every result-returning statement in a .sql file, naming the first qNN_<name>.csv and later ones with _2, _3 suffixes. | Query 1 emits a check table plus two descriptive profiles, and Query 7 mixes a CREATE TABLE with a SELECT; the runner has to handle both without per-file special cases. | Queries 02-07 can be added without touching the runner. |
| 2026-10-04 | The repo-wide name sweep matches whole words rather than bare substrings. | A substring match flags an ordinary logistics word that happens to contain one of the search tokens inside it. Whole-word matching still catches every genuine hit. | Sweep pattern only, no content change. |
| 2026-10-04 | Added a static HTML dashboard under `dashboard/` (vanilla HTML, CSS and JS, no build step and no dependencies), driven by `data/processed/fact_orders.csv`. | The Power BI deliverable ships as an assembly kit with no .pbix file, which leaves the project with no dashboard a reader can actually open. A static page closes that gap honestly and reuses the same canonical metric definitions. | New folder outside the original structure, documented in the README and reconciled against the SQL outputs in the reconciliation phase. |
| 2026-10-04 | The `fact_orders` column contract was fixed centrally before the analysis phase rather than emerging from query 07. | Four consumers read that grain: SQL query 07, the Excel workbook, the DAX measures and the static dashboard. Agreeing the column names and flag semantics up front is what keeps them from drifting apart. | Contract recorded in `powerbi/dax_measures.md` section 1.3 and enforced by CHECK constraints in `database/schema.sql`. |
| 2026-10-04 | Kept the generator's verification parameters as they are even though prescription verification shows almost no pooled association with SLA breach (r = 0.034). | Verification averages roughly 5% of a 24-72 h delivery window, so transit variance genuinely dominates it. Re-tuning the parameters until a stronger correlation appeared would be fitting the data to a desired conclusion. | Management question 3 is answered honestly: no material pooled effect on breach, a visible within-promise-tier gradient, and a strong association with cancellation instead. |
| 2026-10-04 | Queries 02-06 read the three cleaned source tables directly rather than fact_orders. | run_queries.py executes sql/*.sql in filename order, and fact_orders is not populated until 07 runs, so an earlier query reading it would return nothing on a clean rebuild. | Each analysis query is self-contained and the build order carries no hidden dependency. The flag logic is restated per file, which is the cost of that independence. |
| 2026-10-04 | 07_business_summary.sql opens with DELETE FROM fact_orders before the INSERT. | run_queries.py is meant to be re-runnable on its own without rebuilding the database, and a bare INSERT would duplicate all 2,995 rows on the second run. | Re-running the query layer is idempotent; fact_orders.csv is byte-identical across consecutive runs. |
| 2026-10-04 | fact_orders.csv writes the six 0/1 flag columns as integers rather than through the %.2f float format. | Is_On_Time and Is_Late are nullable flags, not measures. Rendering them as 1.00 / 0.00 misrepresents the type and makes the null-off-denominator contract harder to read. | Flags export as 1, 0 or empty; Order_Value, the hour columns and Delay_Hours keep two-decimal formatting. Column names and order are unchanged. |
| 2026-10-04 | The city Priority_Flag rule (bottom-quartile SLA and at-or-above median volume) was kept even though no city satisfies both conditions. | The rule is the question management would ask. An empty result is the answer - the rate problem sits in low-volume Tier 2 cities - and rewriting the rule until it fired would be fitting the test to the data. | Added Late_Share_Pct to the same output so a high-volume, mid-rate city such as Mumbai stays visible on absolute contribution. The report states the flag is empty and why. |
| 2026-10-04 | 05_prescription_analysis.sql reports the pooled verification cut and the within-promised-window cut side by side, with a Sample_Note column rather than row suppression. | The pooled cut is flat because the 24/48/72 h promise grid confounds it, but dropping it would hide the honest headline. Thin within-tier cells are informative as long as their n is on screen. | Management question 3 is answered in three parts - no material pooled effect, a directional within-24h gradient on cells of n=16 to 63, and a strong cancellation association - and labelled segmentation, not causation. |
| 2026-10-04 | Added Late_Refunded_Orders, Late_Refund_Inr and Late_Refund_Inr_Per_Late_Order to the partner x city result set in query 04. | The reallocation scenario in business_findings.md needs refund value for one specific cell. Without the column the figure would have been computed outside the query layer and could not be traced to an output file. | Every number in the scenario arithmetic now resolves to a cell in q04_partner_analysis_2.csv or q02_overall_sla.csv. |

The sqrt() math function is available in the bundled 3.49.1 build, which keeps the calculation in the query layer instead of a side script. | q05_prescription_analysis_3.csv reports sd 1.25 h for verification against 21.07 h for the delivery window, which is the arithmetic behind the claim that transit variance dominates. |
